Table 3 is the central comparison. The SMDP-aware version of DP scores 3.00 ± 0.30 against 3.13 ±

7.3 Does any learned schedule beat the rule?
No learned policy exceeds the rule (return 3.47; one-sample t-tests of the per-replicate returns over 5
replicates). In the SMDP-aware benchmark DP is 0.48 below the rule (p = 0.036), Q-learning 0.26 below
(p = 0.043) and Monte Carlo 0.13 below (p = 0.081). In the naive benchmark the corresponding gaps are
0.34 (p = 0.028), 0.21 (p = 0.130) and 0.67 (p = 0.011). The tests treat the rule's value, a single
deterministic evaluation, as a constant. The logged behaviour policy's modal action scores exactly the
same as the rule in every replicate, so it is omitted from the tables. The learned policies are at best on a
par with the rule that generated their data and mostly worse, a pattern familiar from offline reinforcement
learning [10,15].

Table 5 is the most informative result of the paper, and it is negative for both modelling choices. Even
the oracle model, estimated from 30,000 episodes, predicts that its optimal policy beats the rule by +3.19
(SMDP-aware) or +5.02 (naive); in the simulator the same policies are worse than the rule by 1.15 and
1.11. The sign is wrong in both models, and more data does not fix it: the oracle is trained on ten times
more data than the learners and does worse than they do. The gap is therefore a model gap, not a
sampling gap, and the SMDP correction does not remove it: the two oracle policies are very close in true
return (2.32 versus 2.37). A plausible mechanism, consistent with but not proved by these data, is policy-
induced distribution shift from the omitted hidden stage: the model learns what a state leads to when the
logs came from a risk-stratified rule, and a policy that chooses differently meets the same observed states
with a different mix of hidden stages. We did not test this directly. Estimation optimism is a second
effect: a DP policy trained on 3,000 episodes is predicted by its own model to be 1.84 ± 0.77 (SMDP-
aware) and 2.42 ± 0.92 (naive) better than on the oracle model; the paired difference is +0.58 (p = 0.007).
This is the standard overfitting of plug-in planning.

7.5 Learning dynamics and agreement among solvers




Figure 2. Learning curves on the oracle MDP (SMDP-aware model; mean ± s.d. over replicates). Both learners rise
quickly and plateau below the rule's model value; the oracle DP optimum (dashed) is far above.

Figure 2 shows that learning dynamics are not where the difficulty lies. Both learners start at -2.1 (MC)
and -2.1 (Q-learning) on the oracle MDP, reach 2.98 and 2.82 after only 6,000 steps, and settle at 3.46
and 3.35 after 150,000 (naive benchmark: 3.94 and 4.34, on a different scale because the naive oracle
discounts more mildly). Both end below the rule's model value (3.80) and far below the oracle DP
optimum (6.98): the learners converge to the optimum of their own, imperfect, models, as the diagnostic
table showed.
Q-learning reproduces the DP policy in about 97% of well-visited states (at least 5 visits) and Monte
Carlo in 86%, with nearly the same numbers in the naive model (98% and 87%). Policy iteration and
value iteration returned policies of the same value in 5 of 5 SMDP-aware and 5 of 5 naive replicates, so
the solvers are correct. Agreement between solvers is evidence of convergence, not of the quality of the
model they converge to.
7.6 What the schedules look like
In the stored first replicate the three learned schedules stay close to the behaviour pattern, which is the
rule with noise: they agree with the rule in 87% (DP), 94% (MC) and 87% (Q-learning) of the 108 states.
The oracle DP, in contrast, is very different from the rule: it agrees in only 45% of states (naive oracle:
43%; a random choice would agree in about 33%), which fits the diagnostic above: it is the optimum of a
model whose state omits the disease stage.

Table 6 and Figure 4 give the answer to the question the paper set out to ask. H1 is supported,
monotonically: the two planners choose different intervals in 7.3% of well-visited states at gamma = 0.6,
6.2% at 0.75, 2.6% at 0.9 and only 0.5% at 0.97, consistent with Eq. (5), since the naive overstatement
factor falls from 2.78 to 1.06. H2 is not supported. The return difference is -0.09 ± 0.11 at gamma = 0.6
(p = 0.151), -0.33 ± 0.19 at 0.75 (p = 0.029), -0.33 ± 0.14 at 0.9 (p = 0.009) and +0.01 ± 0.03 at 0.97 (p =
0.374). The sign is negative at 3 of 4 discount factors, so on average the SMDP-aware policy is the worse
one, and nominally significant at 2 of them (gamma = 0.75, 0.9). Against a Bonferroni threshold of
0.0071 only gamma = 0.9 (p = 0.009) survives; the gamma = 0.75 result (p = 0.029) does not. The
gamma = 0.9 entry uses different data seeds and 10,000 rather than 15,000 evaluation episodes, so it is a
separate replication of the main benchmark's DP comparison (-0.13); it points the same way with a larger
magnitude. The difference is not monotone in gamma: it is largest at the middle values and near zero at
the extremes, where at gamma = 0.97 the two policies are almost identical.
What could make a more correct model produce worse policies? We list explanations, none of which we
tested. (a) Compensating errors. The state omits the disease stage, so the estimated transition
probabilities are policy-dependent and biased; the naive model's heavier weighting of the future after a
long interval may offset part of that bias. (b) Proximity to the rule. The naive planner favours long
intervals where the continuation is positive, as the rule does for the numerous low-risk patients. (c)
Noise. A t-based interval would be 1.42 times wider, and the three nominally significant findings are
modest against the multiple-comparison burden. We regard 'the SMDP correction is harmful' as
unproven; 'it is not reliably beneficial' is what the data support.
