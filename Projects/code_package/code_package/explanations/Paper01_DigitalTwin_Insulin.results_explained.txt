Figure 1. Learning curves on the oracle MDP (mean ± s.d. over 5 replicates). Dashed: oracle DP optimum; dotted: rule.

Figure 1 shows learning dynamics. Both solvers jump from near zero to about 10.3 (Q-learning) and 10.3
(Monte Carlo) within the first checkpoint at 4,800 steps and then stop improving: averaged over the last
ten checkpoints Q-learning sits at 9.9 (s.d. across checkpoints and replicates 0.7) and Monte Carlo ends
at 10.3, against 10.27 for the rule and 12.27 for the oracle on the same model. The plateau is set by data,
not by compute. The additional steps only replay a model whose supported cells do not contain a better
policy. Q-learning also oscillates, plausibly because its greedy policy flips between near-equal actions in
cells with few samples (we did not test this).




Figure 2. Dose chosen by each method in each state observed in the oracle data (colour: 0 to 3 units). The learned
policies resemble the behaviour policy; the oracle DP uses 3 u in many more states.

Figure 2 explains why. Of the 36 states, the learned policies differ from the rule in only 13 (DP), 12
(Monte Carlo) and 11 (Q-learning) while the oracle differs in 18; the oracle uses the 3-unit dose in 8
states against 6 for the rule. With ε = 0.25 each non-rule dose is tried in about 6% of visits, so with 3,600
logged transitions (150 days × 24 hours) only the busiest states accumulate the 10 observations needed to
support a non-rule dose. Elsewhere, the support threshold forces the solver back to the rule. This is
intended conservatism, and it caps what 150 days can achieve. Learner agreement with DP in well-

visited states was 92% for Q-learning and 86% for Monte Carlo (the agreement figure is fig_agreement
in the code package), which says that the model-free methods recover the DP policy where there is data
to do so. The optimism gap (value of the DP policy on its own estimated model minus its value on the
oracle model) was positive in 3 of 5 replicates, so estimated models are not reliably optimistic or
pessimistic here.

Table 4 contains the two facts on which the rest of the paper rests. First, a population policy built from
800 days of other patients' logs beats the logged rule at every heterogeneity level: by +1.67 (σ = 0.15),
+1.19 (σ = 0.3), +2.18 (σ = 0.5), +1.73 (σ = 0.8). The pooled policy trained on 800 days across patients
does what the single-patient benchmark could not do with 150 days. (This comparison confounds data
quantity with population diversity, since diverse patients also visit more states and so act as exploration;
the experiments cannot separate the two.) Second, an oracle twin fitted to 2,000 days of the patient's own
data beats the pooled policy by 0.48 to 0.64, with every paired test at p < 0.001, and the oracle twin is the
better policy for 85%, 85%, 100%, 90% of patients (σ = 0.15, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, respectively). The ceiling is
real but modest, roughly 3.4% to 4.5% of the pooled return, and it does not grow monotonically with
heterogeneity: it is 0.48, 0.64, 0.61 and 0.49, with overlapping intervals. We expected the opposite and
did not investigate why. One candidate is that a wider population also gives the pooled model broader
coverage of sensitivities.
The third column group of Table 4 is the cautionary result. A twin built only from the patient's own 7
days is worse than the pooled policy by 1.19 to 2.18 return units at every σ (all p < 0.001), that is, worse
than the pooled policy by more than the oracle twin gains over it. The likely cause is the support
constraint: in 7 of the 16 (σ, days) cells the individual-only policy has exactly the same return as the rule,
which happens when in nearly every state only the rule's own dose has 10 observations, so the planner
has nothing else it may choose. Where it differs (for example at 28 days) it is still no better than the rule:
at σ = 0.15 it scores 12.32 against 12.75. Learning a patient model from days of data, with no prior,
throws away the large amount of information that the population provides.

Figure 4 shows the data-quantity dimension. If more of the patient's own data made the twin better, the
coloured lines would rise toward the dashed black ceiling. They do not. The twins show no consistent
rise toward the oracle with more days; at 28 days several twins fall below the pooled policy (for σ = 0.15:
λ = 10 scores 13.28, pooled 14.42) and their intervals widen sharply. Individual-only (orange) stays on
the rule. A sensible reading is that, in this tabular representation, adding days increases the number of
cells where the patient's counts are just enough to dominate a prior of strength λ but not enough to be
accurate, so more data may move the estimate away from a good prior toward a noisy one (a hypothesis
we did not test).

7.4 Which patients benefit, and what happens to safety




Figure 5. At σ = 0.8 and 7 days: per-patient gain over the pooled policy against the patient's log insulin sensitivity. Left:
shrunk twin (λ = 30). Right: oracle twin (ceiling).

Figure 5 looks at individual patients. In the right-hand panel the ceiling is largest for the least insulin-
sensitive patients (log sensitivity near -1.05, the clipping bound; 4 of 20 test patients sit at that bound)
and moderate elsewhere, with 2 of 20 patients for whom the oracle is not the better policy. In the left-
hand panel the shrunk twin's gain is positive for most of the most insulin-sensitive patients and negative
for a cluster of near-average patients, so it tends to help or harm in different places from the oracle. The
Spearman correlation between the ceiling and the absolute log sensitivity (atypicality) is 0.29 (p =
0.217), which is not distinguishable from zero with 20 patients. We therefore cannot say that atypical
patients benefit most, which is the intuitive hypothesis behind a digital twin; 3 patients sit at the upper
bound 2.5, and clipping may blunt the relationship.

Table 7 reports what the return hides. In-range time follows the return: the oracle twin has the most at
every σ, and the rule and individual-only (which reverts to it) the least. Hypoglycaemia is rare
everywhere, consistent with a heavy reward penalty and a cautious rule, but it is not zero for the
personalised policies. At σ = 0.3 the twin fitted to 28 days spends 0.088 hours per day below 70 mg/dL
against 0.029 for the pooled policy and 0.000 for the rule; at σ = 0.8 the figures are 0.224, 0.019 and
0.000. These are per-day means of small quantities with no CIs stored in the results file, so they are
reported as observed, not tested. They are several times the pooled value in these cells (and every twin
fitted to 14 or 28 days has a higher mean than the pooled policy at every σ), and the pattern reappears at
14 days (σ = 0.3: 0.145 h per day). A twin that doses more aggressively for a patient it believes to be
insensitive exposes that patient to hypoglycaemia whenever the belief is wrong, so a real system would
need an explicit hypoglycaemia constraint, not only a reward penalty.
