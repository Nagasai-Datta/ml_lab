4.2 to 6.0 points above them, and the oracle sits far above. The lower ends of the plotted intervals are
above the rule's value for 2 of the three learners, but these intervals use 1.96 standard errors, which with

is large relative to any systematic gain: more episodes would shrink the intervals, while a better state
representation would raise the ceiling.

Table 4 is the central result. As the number of bins per variable rises from 2 to 8, the state-action space
grows from 72 to 4,608 cells while the data stay fixed; the share of states with any supported action falls
from 95% to 13%, and the median count among observed cells falls from 18.1 to 2.0, which is below the
support threshold of 10. The gain over the rule falls in step: +13.40 ± 7.61, +7.12 ± 4.53, +2.38 ± 1.27
and +0.02 ± 0.03. With t-based intervals the first three all exclude zero, though the g = 2 and g = 3
intervals are wide; the last is positive by a hair and means nothing: at 8 bins the learned policy agrees
with the rule in 99% of well-visited states, and in the remaining states it is the rule by construction.
Agreement with the rule rises from 74% to 99% over the same range, which is the signature of a planner
that has little to say. Mortality moves with the gain, from 5.0% (learned) against 14.3% (rule) at 2 bins to
3.0% against 3.0% at 8 bins.

Figure 4 (left) shows the whole sweep, and the right panel shows the mechanism. The blue line
(coverage) and the green line (gain) fall together while the orange line (agreement with the rule) rises. In
other words, granularity does not make learning harder in an abstract sense; it turns the learned policy
into the rule, state by state, because the data no longer support any deviation. The finest granularity has
the highest absolute return of the sweep (82.4 at g = 8, kappa = 0), but that value is the rule's own (the
rule at 8 bins scores 82.4); no learning is involved. A reader comparing absolute returns across
granularities, which range only from 76.9 to 82.4 at kappa = 0 even though the rule alone ranges from
65.9 to 82.4, would wrongly conclude that fine bins are best for learning. A second caution is that the
coarsest setting offers the largest gain (+13.4) because the two-bin rule is poor, not because two bins is a
good representation: its mortality (14.3% for the rule) is the highest of all rules.

Table 6 gives the paired comparison. Moderate pessimism (kappa = 0.25) is not better than none at any
granularity, and is significantly worse in 3 of 4 (3 bins, 5 bins, 8 bins): -0.98 (p = 0.267) at 2 bins, -6.98
(p = 0.019) at 3 bins, -2.54 (p = 0.020) at 5 bins, -0.33 (p < 0.001) at 8 bins. The right-hand columns
explain why a penalty is redundant. The model value of the chosen policy is below its true value in every
one of the 24 sweep settings (the table shows the replicate mean at kappa = 0 and 0.25), and by a large
margin as granularity increases (-49.8 at 5 bins and -121.6 at 8). The reason is that unsupported pairs are
completed with the worst observed reward and an absorbing state, which is already a strong form of
pessimism. When most pairs are unsupported the model undervalues almost every policy, and a second
penalty only moves the planner further toward the best-observed action. The right measure of danger in
this setting is therefore not over-optimism (the model is already pessimistic) but pessimism so severe that
the planner has nothing to choose among.




Figure 5. Left: true return against kappa for each granularity (dotted lines: the rule at that granularity; bars: 95% CI).
Right: simulated in-ICU mortality against kappa.

Figure 5 shows the same sweep on the return scale and adds mortality. For 3 bins the return drops from
about 77 at kappa = 0 to about 70 at kappa = 0.25 and then stays flat at the rule's level; mortality moves
from 6.7% to 8.8%. For 2 bins the line is flat until kappa = 0.1, falls to 68.7 at kappa = 0.5 and to 63.8 at
kappa = 1.0, below the rule (65.9), and its mortality climbs from 5.0% to 15.5%, above the rule's 14.3%.
A penalty that is large relative to the +100 and −100 terminal rewards (the penalty at n = 10 is about 0.32
× kappa × Δr, which is of order 60 × kappa when Δr = 200) can make ending the episode quickly
attractive, and the rising mortality at 2 bins is consistent with that mechanism; we did not test it directly,
so it remains a hypothesis. The tight intervals at 5 and 8 bins show how little variation a mostly
unsupported model produces.
