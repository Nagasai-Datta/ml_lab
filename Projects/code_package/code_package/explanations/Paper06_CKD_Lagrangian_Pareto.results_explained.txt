Table 3 shows that all three learners beat the rule at unit price: DP 13.97 ± 0.15, Q-learning 13.60 ± 0.19
and MC 13.15 ± 0.38 against 11.80. Paired over replicates the differences from the rule are +2.17 ± 0.15
(p < 0.001) for DP, +1.80 ± 0.19 (p < 0.001) for Q-learning and +1.34 ± 0.38 (p = 0.002) for MC. The
solvers are ranked DP above Q-learning (paired difference +0.37 ± 0.23, p = 0.033) above MC (DP
minus MC +0.82 ± 0.48, p = 0.028). The gain is bought by trading burden for failures: the learners cut
burden from 6.86 to 3.18 (DP) but raise the simulated kidney-failure rate from 4.0% to 11.6%. Whether
that is a good trade depends entirely on the price λ = 1, which is a modelling choice, so the improvement
is real only relative to that price. The constant 'always enhanced' policy, evaluated on 600 episodes,
scores 13.83 at this price (always routine 11.72, always intensive 8.14), so a learned policy that is
statistically ahead of the rule is not clearly ahead of the best constant policy. The oracle DP, fitted to
6,000 episodes, scored 13.91, which is within 0.06 of DP fitted to 800 episodes; the tabular
representation, not data volume, limits performance here (the oracle model's own value for its policy is
16.86, 3.0 above its true value; we did not diagnose the cause, which may be state aggregation or the
stationary treatment of the horizon).

Figure 1 shows that both learners start near 13.1 on the oracle model, already above the rule's 11.4, and

Little learning is visible in the curves: the start is above the rule because the initial greedy policy (we did

24 (Q-learning) of 120 states, and the oracle in 55; Q-learning agreed with DP in 91% of well-visited
states and MC in 77% (Figure 2). The DP policy's model value minus its true value (optimism gap) was
positive in 2 of 5 replicates, so on average the model is not systematically optimistic about the policy it
picks, in contrast to its optimism about the oracle's.

Figure 2 shows that the learners keep the behaviour policy's coarse structure (intensive at low eGFR,
routine at high eGFR) and change many individual cells. Because the cells are sorted by eGFR band, the
striping reveals that the changes depend on proteinuria and previous action, as the state encodes, but also
that many are isolated single cells, which look like noise from thin data. Over all 120 states the oracle

gives much less intensive care than the rule, which is what a unit price on burden rewards, and it departs

Table 4 is the frontier. As the price rises from 0 to 16, burden falls from 6.23 to 0.21, benefit falls from
18.39 to 12.14, and the kidney-failure fraction rises from 7.5% to 35.8%; all three are strictly monotone
in the price, as theory predicts. At the top price the policy is nearly the always-routine policy (benefit
11.72, burden 0.00, failure 38.5%). The exchange rate in the last column increases overall with the price
(from 0.45 to 2.80), which is the signature of a concave frontier: the first units of burden saved are cheap
in benefit and the last are expensive. Equation (4) requires each rate to lie between the two prices that
bound it; it does for 3 of the 7 pairs, and fails for 0 to 0.25 (0.45, above the upper price), 2 to 4 (1.77,
below the lower price), 4 to 8 (2.45, below the lower price), 8 to 16 (2.80, below the lower price). At the
high-price end, where the realised rate falls short of the price, the learned policy at the higher price is not
optimal for the lower price in the true simulator.




Figure 3. Benefit against burden: the Lagrangian DP frontier (points labelled by price; bars: 95% CI over 10 replicates)
and the four reference policies.

Figure 3 puts the frontier next to the fixed policies. The 'always enhanced' policy sits above the frontier:
it achieves benefit 17.36 at burden 3.53, whereas linear interpolation between learned points gives 16.99
at that burden, a shortfall of 0.37, larger than the frontier's own interval widths (0.15 to 0.21 at the
neighbouring prices). The nephrologist-style rule lies beyond the right end of the frontier: it has burden
6.79 against 6.23 for the most benefit-seeking learned policy (λ = 0), and higher benefit (18.84 against
18.39) and a lower failure rate (3.5% against 7.5%). 'Always intensive' reaches benefit 20.45 at burden
12.31, far above anything the learner explores. In other words, the learned frontier is limited by the
support of the logs: with λ = 0 a benefit-maximising planner would give intensive care everywhere, but it
can do so only in states where the logs contain enough intensive visits, and those are the low-eGFR states
where the rule already does it. That reading is a hypothesis about the mechanism, supported by the
behaviour policy's structure (Figure 2) but not tested separately.

Figure 4 shows the safety cost of pricing burden. The failure fraction rises slowly from 7.5% at λ = 0 to
11.5% at λ = 0.5, then steeply to 28.8% at λ = 4 and flattens near 35.8%, close to the always-routine rate
(38.5%). A failure costs 20 benefit units, comparable to the whole discounted benefit of a surviving
patient (Table 4), so this curve is the part of the frontier that a clinician would worry about: a price of 1
already multiplies the failure rate by 2.2 relative to λ = 0 (16.3% versus 7.5%), and the rule's 3.5% is
below every learned point (the lowest is 7.5%).

margin to 3, 0 and 0. The calibration rule of Eq. (6) predicts that a margin of at least ρ − 1, that is 39%,

Figure 5. Realised burden (left; black bars: budget) and benefit (right) by budget and margin (10 replicates; 95% CI).

Figure 5 shows the same in the true-simulator units. The mean realised burden shrinks with the margin:
at a 25% margin it is 2.12, 3.99 and 5.81 for budgets 2, 4 and 6, and at 50% it is 1.85, 3.46 and 4.87. A
mean at or just under the budget still leaves many replicates above it, because replicates scatter around
the mean (the error bars), which is why exceedances persist at 25%. The price of safety is visible on the
right: a 50% margin instead of none costs 0.86, 0.79 and 0.58 units of benefit and raises the kidney-
failure fraction by 3.8, 3.5 and 2.8 percentage points at budgets 2, 4 and 6. Looser budgets are cheaper to
protect than tight ones, consistent with a concave frontier: the same proportional tightening removes a
smaller slice of benefit where the frontier is flatter.




Figure 6. Share of replicates whose realised burden exceeded the budget, by budget and margin (10 replicates).

Figure 6 summarises the exceedance rates as percentages. With 10 replicates each point has a granularity
of 10 percentage points and a wide binomial interval, so the figure should be read as monotone evidence
(more margin, fewer violations) and not as a precise rate. Even with a 50% margin the budget of 2 was
exceeded in 3 of 10 replicates, which a user who needs a hard guarantee would not accept; a distribution-
free guarantee would require a conformal-style or hold-out calibration, which we did not do.
