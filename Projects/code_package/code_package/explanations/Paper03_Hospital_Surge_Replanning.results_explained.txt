Table 3 shows that planning on the estimated MDP essentially reproduces the occupancy rule: DP scores
99.5 ± 0.9 against 100.5 for the rule, and even the oracle DP (7.5 times more data) scores 99.8. This is
not a failure of the solver - the DP and policy-iteration solutions agreed in every replicate - but a property
of the problem: the rule was written by someone who understood it, so a policy that is optimal for the
estimated, state-aggregated model has little left to find. Q-learning is below the rule (96.3 ± 2.1). Monte
Carlo is clearly worse (86.4 ± 3.5); its policies divert about 10.7 patients per episode against 1.0 for the
rule, presumably because diversion has a high-variance, rarely-observed outcome that the episode-level
returns estimate noisily.

7.2 Demand surge: every baseline-trained policy fails
We now raise arrivals by 30% without changing anything else. Table 4 (below) shows that a policy
trained on baseline data - or the occupancy rule itself - collapses: the static policy's return falls from 98.5
(baseline) to -9.9 ± 1.4 under surge, and the rule scores -10.5. A policy trained on an abundance of surge
data (6,000 episodes) obtains 28.3. The gap of about 38 points is the value of knowing the regime; it is
also an upper bound on what any adaptation scheme can recover from a few weeks of data.

7.3 Adaptation: how much surge data, and how to use it




Figure 4. Return under surge as surge episodes accumulate. Recency-weighted pooling (ω = 10) improves with more
data, though noisily; equal-weight pooling needs many more episodes; using surge data alone does not help (nominally
below the static policy). Error bars: 95% CI over 6 replicates.

Figure 4 is the central result. Four observations follow. (1) Recency weighting works. With ω = 10 the
return improves from -9.9 to -6.5 after only 5 surge episodes (140 days) and to -1.8 after 20; at 20
episodes the improvement over the static policy is 8.1 ± 3.6 (p = 0.007, paired over replicates). At 80
episodes it reaches 3.8, although the confidence interval (± 4.9) is wide. (2) Equal-weight pooling is
slow. The 800 baseline episodes swamp the new data; with ω = 1 nothing changes until 40 episodes (-
6.5) and the return is still -2.9 at 80. (3) Surge data alone does not help. With ω → ∞ and the old data
removed, the return is -11.4 at 5 episodes and -12.7 at 80, nominally below the static policy (-9.9); the
paired difference at 20 episodes is -1.4 ± 1.4 (p = 0.106), so we cannot call it harmful, only not helpful.
At 5 episodes the recency-weighted variant beats it by 4.9 ± 3.0 (p = 0.024). The surge-only return is
identical across replicates at 5, 10 and 20 episodes (zero spread), which suggests that the support
threshold leaves almost all of the 1,470 state-action pairs unsupported so that the policy falls back to the
clinician rule (we did not verify this directly); its baseline return is also not reduced (99.3), consistent
with that reading. (4) The remaining gap is large. Even at 80 episodes, adaptation recovers only about
36% of the 38-point gap to the oracle (and about 21% at 20 episodes); the oracle used 75 times more
surge data.

Figure 5. Regret check: return under the original demand for the same policies. Recency-weighted replanning gives up

Figure 5 shows the price of adapting. If demand returns to normal, the recency-weighted policy built
from 80 surge episodes scores 94.2 instead of 98.5, a regret of 4.3. Equal-weight pooling loses less
(97.3), which is the mirror image of its slower adaptation, and surge-only replanning loses nothing here.
The regret curves have no error bars (means of 6 replicates). The two figures together define a frontier:
more weight on new data buys return under the surge and costs return in the old regime.
